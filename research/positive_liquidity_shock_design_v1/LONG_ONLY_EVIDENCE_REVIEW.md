# Long-only evidence review

## Verdict

`POSITIVE_LIQUIDITY_SHOCK_LONG_LEG_SUPPORTED`

The verdict applies to the original broad diversified portfolios, not to a
top-six or one-name implementation.

## Original one-month result

Bali et al. Table 3 (p.1449), percent per month:

| Portfolio | EW return | EW FF3 alpha | VW return | VW FF3 alpha |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Low LIQU decile | 0.51 | -0.75 | 0.43 | -0.73 |
| Middle D5/D6 | 1.08 / 1.07 | -0.02 / -0.02 | 0.99 / 1.04 | -0.01 / 0.04 |
| High LIQU decile | 1.69 | 0.48 | 1.60 | 0.45 |
| High minus low | 1.18 | 1.23 | 1.17 | 1.17 |

High-leg alpha is independently significant: EW t=6.88 and VW t=6.01. The
high decile also exceeds the middle portfolios materially. This is not merely
a negative-leg anomaly.

It is nevertheless asymmetric. Using FF3 alphas, roughly 39% of the EW spread
comes from the high leg (`0.48/1.23`) and 61% from low-leg underperformance.
The decile pattern is broadly, but not strictly, monotone.

The Internet Appendix midpoint-return test preserves high-minus-low returns
and alphas, weakening a bid–ask-bounce explanation. The paper's continuation
through about six months is regression evidence; it does not define six
separate concentrated holding-period strategies. The canonical portfolio
formation remains one month.

## Concentration boundary

Every result is a decile/quintile portfolio or cross-sectional regression.
Nothing in the paper estimates the order-statistic reliability of the top six
or the single most extreme issuer. The attractive long leg is real in-sample,
but its evidence is diversified.
